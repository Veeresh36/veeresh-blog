# Doraemon, Chhota Bheem, Shinchan, Oggy: The Cartoons That Quietly Taught Me How to Think

> *I still catch myself watching Doraemon on a random weeknight. Not for nostalgia. Because it still gives me ideas.*

**Published:** June 30, 2026 · **11 min read** · By [Veeresh Bashetti](https://veereshbashetti.com)

---

I want to admit something that feels strangely vulnerable to type out as an adult.

I still watch Doraemon.

Not occasionally, out of nostalgia, on some lazy Sunday when nothing else is on. I genuinely sit through an episode sometimes, fully present, fully curious — the same way I used to as a kid sitting cross-legged in front of the TV with a plate of food going cold beside me.

And every single time, something strange happens. I get curious. I get ideas. My brain starts doing that thing it does when I'm reading a good book or debugging an interesting problem — that quiet, restless "what if" feeling.

It made me stop and ask a real question: how did a children's cartoon do that?

Not just Doraemon. Chhota Bheem. Shinchan. Oggy and the Cockroaches. Ben 10. Mr. Bean. Ninja Hattori. These were not background noise during our childhood evenings, even though that's how I treated them at the time. Looking back at them now, as someone who spends most working days writing code and solving problems for a living, I can trace specific habits of mine — how I approach a stuck problem, how I show up for people, how I let myself think in rough, unfiltered drafts before polishing anything — directly back to these shows.

This is not a nostalgia listicle. This is the honest, first-hand account of what these seven cartoons actually gave me, character by character, and the one quiet lesson sitting underneath every single one of them.

---

## Doraemon: The Art of Asking "What If" Before Asking "How"

If there is one character who shaped my imagination more than any teacher, any book, any school project — it might genuinely be Doraemon.

Here was a robotic cat from the future who pulled an impossible gadget out of a pocket for almost every problem a child could imagine. A door that opens anywhere. A copy machine for objects. A tool that shrinks or grows anything you point it at.

As a kid, I watched these gadgets as magic. As an adult rewatching them, I see something different — a structure of thinking. Every episode starts the same way: a problem, ordinary and human, followed by a wildly imaginative "what if" solution. What if you could fix every mistake by going back in time? What if distance was not a real obstacle at all? What if you could see exactly how a choice today changes your future?

That habit — asking "what if" before asking "how is this even possible" — is the exact same mental motion behind every invention, every piece of art, every good idea I've ever had as an adult. When I'm stuck on a hard problem at work now, I genuinely notice myself doing a smaller, quieter version of what Doraemon's pocket used to do: instead of asking "what's the standard way to fix this," I ask "what if I looked at this completely differently." Doraemon did not teach me facts. It trained a kind of imaginative curiosity that I still rely on, every time I sit down to build something new.

There's also a part of the story most people forget once they grow out of it: Nobita, the boy Doraemon helps, is not naturally gifted. He's lazy, he fails often, and he gets bailed out constantly by gadgets he doesn't fully deserve. But the episodes that stuck with me most as an adult are the rare ones where Nobita solves something *without* a gadget — through sheer stubbornness. That contrast quietly taught me that having a shortcut available doesn't mean you should always reach for it.

## Chhota Bheem: Courage Doesn't Need to Be Loud

Chhota Bheem gave an entire generation of Indian kids something quietly powerful — the idea that courage and kindness could exist in the same small body.

He wasn't the biggest character in Dholakpur, and he wasn't the loudest. Kalia was louder. Raju was quicker with a clever line. But Bheem showed up, again and again, for his friends and his village, usually with nothing more than a plate of laddoos and a stubborn refusal to let people he cared about get hurt.

What I notice now, watching old episodes with younger cousins, is how rarely Bheem's strength is framed as the point of the story. The point is almost always loyalty — he shows up before he's asked to, and he stays after the danger is gone, helping rebuild whatever got broken along the way. That's a strange thing for a "superhero-adjacent" kids' show to prioritize over raw strength, and it's exactly why the lesson outlived the show for me.

Looking back now, the lesson feels almost embarrassingly simple, and that's exactly why it stuck: you don't need to be the strongest person in the room to protect what matters to you. You just need to show up, consistently, for the people and the things you believe in. As an adult, that's shown up for me less as physical courage and more as the smaller version of it — staying on a hard project when it would be easier to quietly disengage, or being the person who follows up when everyone else has moved on.

## Shinchan and Oggy: Permission to Be a Little Ridiculous

Shinchan and Oggy and the Cockroaches get treated like the "silly" entries on this list, the ones nobody expects to learn anything from. I'd push back on that a little, because rewatching both as an adult taught me two distinct things, not one.

**Shinchan's** entire personality was built on saying exactly what he was thinking, without the filter most adults spend years building. It was funny, sure — sometimes uncomfortably so, which is part of why it worked. But there was something underneath the chaos — a reminder that not every thought needs to be polished and pre-approved before it's allowed to exist. Some of my better ideas as an adult have come from exactly that kind of unfiltered, slightly ridiculous first draft of a thought — the version I'd never say out loud in a meeting, but that often contains the seed of something genuinely useful once I clean it up.

**Oggy and the Cockroaches**, on the other hand, taught a different kind of lesson — one about patience and persistence wrapped entirely in slapstick humor, with almost no dialogue at all. No matter how many times Joey, Dee Dee and Marky destroyed Oggy's house, the story reset, and life simply continued. As a kid, that just meant more chaos tomorrow. As an adult, it reads like a quiet metaphor I didn't expect a cartoon about cockroaches to teach me: things go wrong constantly, repeatedly, and almost comically, and the only real choice you have is whether you get back up for the next round. There's a kind of resilience training hidden inside something that looks like pure slapstick.

---

## Ben 10: Curiosity About Becoming Someone New

Ben 10 worked on a simple but genuinely powerful idea — a device, the Omnitrix, that let an ordinary kid transform into something completely different whenever the moment demanded it.

What stuck with me wasn't the aliens or the action sequences, even though those were the reason I watched in the first place. It was the underlying curiosity about identity — the idea that you are not just one fixed version of yourself. Depending on the challenge in front of you, you can choose to become more patient, more bold, more thoughtful, more disciplined. Ben rarely knew exactly which form would solve the problem until he tried, and he got it wrong constantly before getting it right.

Neither do we, most of the time. I've noticed this directly in how I approach hard weeks at work: some problems need the "patient, slow-debugging" version of me, others need the "just ship something and iterate" version, and a few genuinely need the version of me that's comfortable saying "I don't know, let's find out together." We just have to be willing to try a different version of ourselves when the one we're currently using isn't working, the same way Ben kept slapping a new alien onto a stuck problem until something finally clicked.

---

## Mr. Bean: Connection Doesn't Require Words

Mr. Bean is the strangest entry on this list, because he barely speaks at all — and yet somehow communicates more clearly than half the dialogue-heavy shows from the same era.

Every expression, every exaggerated reaction, every small disaster he creates for himself tells a complete story without a single line of explanation needed. As someone who now spends a large part of his life writing and explaining things in words — for work, for this blog, for documentation nobody asked for but everyone needs — I find that genuinely humbling. Mr. Bean is proof that clarity and connection are not about how much you say. They're about how honestly you say it, even if "saying it" means nothing more than a single raised eyebrow or a comically over-engineered solution to a problem that needed none of it.

There's a quieter lesson in there too about over-complication. Mr. Bean routinely takes the longest, strangest, most elaborate route to solve a simple problem — and watching that as an adult, I recognize the same instinct in myself when I'm building something and reach for a complicated solution before trying the obvious one.

---

## Ninja Hattori: Quiet Discipline Hidden Inside Fun

Ninja Hattori always felt a little different from the rest — calmer, more disciplined, more deliberate in how he moved through problems, even while wrapped in the same comedic, kid-friendly tone as everything else on this list.

Underneath the ninja gadgets and the comedy was a quieter message about discipline: skill isn't just talent, it's practiced, repeated, and applied calmly even when the situation around you is chaotic. Hattori never panicked, even when Kenichi got himself into trouble for the hundredth time. He moved with the same calm precision whether the stakes were a missing homework assignment or an actual threat.

It's easy to miss that lesson as a kid, distracted by the action and the gadgets. It's much harder to miss it as an adult, once you've actually had to build a skill the slow, unglamorous way — through repetition that doesn't feel exciting in the moment, the same way Hattori's discipline never looked exciting on screen, just reliable.

## Why Revisiting These Cartoons Still Gives Me Ideas Today

Here is the honest answer to the question I asked at the start of this post.

These cartoons still give me ideas because they were never really written for children alone. They were written by people who understood something timeless about curiosity, courage, humor, identity and discipline, and then wrapped that understanding in a story simple enough for a child to follow without realizing they were being taught anything at all.

As a kid, I watched the surface story. As an adult, rewatching the same episodes, I notice the structure underneath it, and that structure is genuinely useful to me in ways I didn't expect when I started writing this post. It's the same structure behind a good product idea, a good piece of writing, a good solution to a hard problem. Ask an honest "what if," the way Doraemon's gadgets always did. Show up for the people who matter, the way Bheem did without needing to be asked twice. Let your unfiltered first thought exist before you polish it, the way Shinchan never hesitated to. Get back up after the chaos, the way Oggy's house somehow always did. Be willing to become a different version of yourself when the moment calls for it, the way Ben never knew which alien would work until he tried. Say less, but mean it more, the way Mr. Bean never needed a single sentence to be understood. Practice calmly, even in chaos, the way Hattori always did without anyone noticing how hard that actually is.

None of that was ever really about cartoons. It was always about how to think — and once you notice it, you can't really unnotice it again.

## The One Moral That Ties All of It Together

If I had to compress everything above into a single idea — the one thing every one of these shows quietly agreed on, despite being made by completely different people in completely different countries, decades apart from one another — it would be this:

**Curiosity, kindness and showing up consistently will always solve more problems than power, perfection or shortcuts ever could.**

Doraemon never solved a single problem by force; only by imagination. Chhota Bheem never won by being the strongest; only by being loyal. Shinchan and Oggy never had it together; that was the entire point. Ben 10 never had one fixed answer; only the willingness to try a different version of himself. Mr. Bean never needed words; only honesty. Ninja Hattori never relied on talent alone; only quiet, consistent discipline.

That's the moral. Not hidden in some grand finale episode, but sitting quietly underneath every single one of them, episode after episode, for years, without us ever noticing we were being taught anything at all. And somewhere between growing up and being handed adult responsibilities, most of us were quietly taught that curiosity is for children, that imagination is something you "grow out of," and that revisiting old cartoons is a small, slightly embarrassing indulgence.

I don't think that's true anymore. The more time I spend building things, whether that's writing, code, or just trying to solve a hard problem in my own life, the more I notice how often the instinct that helps me starts in the exact same place these cartoons trained when I was seven years old: a simple, stubborn "what if."

So if you ever find yourself, like I do, watching an old episode of Doraemon late at night and feeling that same restless curiosity you felt as a child, don't treat it as a guilty pleasure. Treat it as proof that the most useful part of your mind was built a long time ago, by stories far simpler than you'd expect, and it's still right there, waiting for you to use it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do childhood cartoons like Doraemon still feel meaningful as adults?

Cartoons like Doraemon work on two levels at once — a simple, fun story for a child, and a quiet lesson about curiosity, friendship, or problem-solving underneath it. As adults, we no longer notice the surface story as much, but we recognize the lesson, which is why rewatching them can feel surprisingly reflective rather than just nostalgic.

### Can watching cartoons actually improve creativity and imagination?

Yes. Cartoons that build entire imaginative worlds — gadgets that solve impossible problems, characters who think differently, or simple humor with no dialogue at all — train the brain to ask "what if" questions. That habit of imaginative questioning is the same mental muscle used in invention, design, writing and problem-solving.

### What is the difference between watching cartoons for entertainment and learning from them?

The difference is reflection. Entertainment is watching and moving on. Learning is noticing why a character's choice mattered, what value the story was quietly built around, and carrying that thought into your own life later. The cartoons stay the same — it is our attention that decides which one we get.

### Is it normal for adults to still enjoy cartoons from their childhood?

It is completely normal and increasingly common. Adults often return to childhood shows during stressful or uncertain periods because these stories offer comfort, simplicity, and a reminder of who they were before adult pressures shaped them. It is less about the cartoon and more about reconnecting with a calmer version of your own mind.

### What is one common life lesson across most classic children's cartoons?

Almost every great children's cartoon, regardless of genre or country, circles back to one idea: kindness and curiosity solve more problems than power or shortcuts ever do. Whether it's a gadget from the future, a forest of animal friends, or a child asking endless questions, the underlying moral rarely changes — only the costume does.

### Which cartoon character teaches the most useful real-life lesson?

It depends on what you need most. Doraemon teaches imaginative problem-solving, Chhota Bheem teaches quiet courage, Shinchan and Oggy teach resilience and unfiltered honesty, Ben 10 teaches adaptability, Mr. Bean teaches expressive clarity without words, and Ninja Hattori teaches calm discipline. Most people find that one of these resonates more strongly depending on what stage of life or work they are currently in.
