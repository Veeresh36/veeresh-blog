# 18 Free Websites That Feel Illegal to Know (But Are 100% Legal) — 2026 Edition

## Why I Rewrote This List Instead of Just Reposting It

You've probably seen the graphic. Black background, bold blue text, "20 WEBSITES GOOGLE DOESN'T WANT YOU TO KNOW ABOUT," a neatly numbered list, and a caption asking you to comment a keyword to get the links. It gets reshared constantly because a lot of the entries are genuinely excellent — free tools that quietly do the job of software people pay for.

But a few entries on lists like that one aren't clever discoveries. Sites like **libgen.is**, **sci-hub.se**, and **pdfdrive.com** exist specifically to distribute copyrighted books and research papers without the rights holder's permission — that's not a loophole, it's copyright infringement, and publishers have taken legal action over exactly these sites. A paywall-bypass tool like 12ft.io sits in similarly murky territory, since it's built to strip out the access controls a publisher put on content it owns.

So instead of just reposting the graphic, I went through every entry, kept only what's genuinely free and legal, checked whether it still works the way people claim in 2026, and swapped in a couple of tools I actually use every week. Eighteen made the final cut.

## Research & Archiving

### 1. Wayback Machine (archive.org)

Run by the nonprofit Internet Archive since 1996, the Wayback Machine has archived over 800 billion web pages and is completely free, with no signup required. Type in any URL, pick a date on the calendar, and you can browse what that page looked like years ago — useful for recovering a deleted article, checking what a company's site said before it changed its story, or citing a source that's since gone offline. There's also a "Save Page Now" button so you can archive a page yourself before it disappears.

**Best for:** journalists, researchers, and anyone who needs to prove what a webpage said on a specific date.

### 2. Project Gutenberg (gutenberg.org)

Over 70,000 free ebooks — all public domain, meaning the copyright has expired and the books are legally free to download, no gray areas involved. Classic literature, philosophy, and reference works in EPUB, Kindle, and plain text formats. If you want a free book that's still under copyright, Project Gutenberg isn't the place — check your library's Libby or OverDrive app instead, which lends current ebooks legally through your library card.

**Best for:** classic literature, research citations, free audiobook source material.

### 3. Open Culture (openculture.com)

A curated directory of free courses, audiobooks, movies, and textbooks from universities like MIT, Stanford, and Yale — all legitimately released as open courseware or public domain material by the institutions themselves. It's a directory, not a host, so you're always clicking through to the official source.

**Best for:** self-taught learners who want university-level material without university tuition.

### 4. WolframAlpha (wolframalpha.com)

A "computational knowledge engine" rather than a search engine — type in a math problem, a chemistry formula, a unit conversion, or a statistics question, and it computes an actual answer with steps, rather than linking you to a page that might have one. The free version covers most day-to-day and student use; a paid Pro tier adds step-by-step solutions for more complex problems.

**Best for:** checking homework, quick calculations, unit conversions, data lookups.

## Image & Design Tools

### 5. Photopea (photopea.com)

A free, browser-based image editor that reads and edits PSD, XD, Sketch, and Figma files directly — essentially a Photoshop clone that needs no installation and no account. It includes a one-click Remove Background tool alongside full layer and mask support, and because it runs entirely in your browser, your images generally aren't uploaded to a server. It's genuinely free with ads; there's no forced trial or paywall for the core editor.

**Best for:** editing PSD files without a Photoshop subscription, quick multi-layer edits.

### 6. Squoosh (squoosh.app)

Google's own free image compression tool. Drag in a JPG, PNG, or WebP, compare compression settings side-by-side in real time, and export a much smaller file without a visible quality drop. It runs locally in your browser, so nothing gets uploaded — genuinely useful before adding images to a website (this blog included).

**Best for:** shrinking image file sizes for faster-loading websites.

### 7. remove.bg

The tool most people mean when they say "AI background remover." Upload a photo and it isolates the subject in seconds, including tricky details like hair. The free tier gives you a full-resolution preview but caps the actual download at a lower resolution — for full-res exports or batch processing you'll need a paid plan or API key. Still one of the fastest, most accurate free options for a quick cutout.

**Best for:** product photos, profile pictures, quick transparent PNGs.

### 8. Cleanup.pictures

Point-and-erase object removal — draw over a person, watermark, or stray object in a photo, and the AI fills in the background convincingly. Handy for cleaning up a busy background in a photo you're about to use for a resume, LinkedIn post, or listing.

**Best for:** removing unwanted objects or people from photos without opening a full editor.

### 9. Unscreen (unscreen.com)

The video equivalent of remove.bg — upload a short video and it strips the background out automatically, frame by frame, no green screen required. It works best on short, well-lit clips with a clear subject; for longer or more demanding footage, expect to test a couple of alternatives before you find one that handles hair and motion cleanly, since this category has gotten more competitive in 2026.

**Best for:** quick video background removal for short clips, reaction videos, presentations.

### 10. Unsplash (unsplash.com)

A massive library of free, high-resolution stock photos released under a license that permits commercial use without attribution (crediting the photographer is appreciated, not required). It's the single fastest way to get a genuinely good hero image for a blog post or presentation without touching a paid stock site.

**Best for:** blog headers, presentation backgrounds, mockup photos.

### 11. Coolors (coolors.co)

A free color palette generator — hit the spacebar to generate palettes, lock the colors you like, and export hex codes instantly. Useful for anyone designing a site, deck, or brand kit without a design background.

**Best for:** quickly generating a coherent color palette for any project.

## Developer & Creator Tools

### 12. Carbon (carbon.now.sh)

Turns a plain code snippet into a polished, shareable image — pick a theme, a background gradient, and a language, and it renders clean syntax-highlighted code as a PNG or SVG. Popular for sharing code on Twitter/X, in blog posts, or in presentation slides.

**Best for:** making code snippets look presentable in social posts and docs.

### 13. Ray.so

A faster, more minimal alternative to Carbon, built by the Raycast team — same idea (turn code into a clean image), with a slightly different set of themes and export options. Worth trying both and picking whichever's default style you prefer.

**Best for:** quick code screenshots with a more minimal aesthetic.

### 14. Shots.so

Free device mockups — drop a screenshot into a phone, laptop, or browser frame and export it instantly, no design software required. Useful for making an app screenshot or website preview look presentation-ready in seconds.

**Best for:** portfolio pieces, product pages, and app store screenshots.

### 15. Smartmockups (smartmockups.com)

A larger mockup library than Shots.so, covering devices, apparel, packaging, and print materials — the free tier gives you a solid number of mockups and exports per month before it asks you to upgrade, so it's worth using for occasional needs even though it's not unlimited.

**Best for:** product mockups when you need something beyond a simple device frame.

## Everyday Utility

### 16. AlternativeTo (alternativeto.net)

A crowdsourced directory for finding free or cheaper alternatives to paid software — search "Photoshop" and it surfaces Photopea, GIMP, and others, with user ratings and platform filters (Windows, Mac, web, open source). Genuinely useful before you commit to a subscription.

**Best for:** researching free/cheaper alternatives before buying software.

### 17. JustWatch (justwatch.com)

A free search engine for streaming — type in a movie or show and it tells you exactly which services (Netflix, Prime Video, Disney+, and so on) have it available to stream, rent, or buy in your country, instead of you opening five apps to check.

**Best for:** finding where to legally stream something without app-hopping.

### 18. Have I Been Pwned (haveibeenpwned.com)

Built by independent security researcher Troy Hunt in 2013, this is the reference tool for checking whether your email address has appeared in a known data breach. The core site is free — search an email, see which breaches it turned up in and what data was exposed, and sign up for free automatic alerts for future breaches. It doesn't store the email you search, and its separate Pwned Passwords tool lets you check whether a password has been exposed using a method that never transmits your full password. (A paid API key is only needed for bulk or programmatic domain searches — regular manual lookups stay free.)

**Best for:** checking your own exposure after a breach headline, before you reuse a password anywhere.

## A Quick Word on What Didn't Make the List

If you came here from that "20 websites" graphic wondering why your favorite four are missing, here's the honest answer:

- **libgen.is / Library Genesis** and **sci-hub.se** host copyrighted books and academic papers without authorization from the authors or publishers. Multiple publishers have won court judgments against them. If you need a paper, try Google Scholar's "All versions" link, PubMed Central, your university's library access, or emailing the author directly — most researchers will happily send you a copy.
- **pdfdrive.com** operates the same way for books — a large share of its catalog is copyrighted material shared without permission. Project Gutenberg (public domain) and your library's Libby/OverDrive app (legally licensed current titles) cover the same need legally.
- **12ft.io** and similar paywall-stripping tools circumvent access controls that publishers put on content they own, which raises both legal and ethical issues, and can breach a site's terms of service. If you read a publication regularly, a subscription funds the actual reporting; if it's occasional, many sites offer a handful of free reads a month, and a library card often unlocks free digital access to major papers.

None of this is a lecture — it's just the reasoning for why this list looks a little different from the graphic that inspired it.
