There were four of us. My friends, laughing, present, fully inside the day. And me — physically there, mentally somewhere else, running a number I couldn't quite name.

*What am I getting from this?*

Not "am I happy" or "am I sad." Those felt like the wrong questions. The real question sitting underneath everything was quieter and stranger: **what's the profit here?**

I didn't choose that word by accident. It's the word that came to me in the moment, and I think it's worth taking seriously instead of correcting into something more poetic. Profit means return. It means something goes in and something measurable comes out. And somewhere along the way, that became the lens I hold up to my own life — even to a road trip with people I care about.

## The Feeling of Watching From Outside

I sat with that thought for the whole trip. Not brooding exactly — just watching. Watching my friends enjoy the scenery, the music, each other. Watching myself watch them. There's a strange double vision that happens when you're present in your body but absent in your attention — you can describe every detail of the moment later, and still tell someone honestly, "I wasn't really there."

Later, at a movie, the same static returned. Different setting, same signal: low. Not sad-low. Just... flat. The screen was doing its job. I wasn't doing mine.

And here's the part that actually explains the flatness: **I only seem to feel something when I've achieved something.** Joy, for me, has quietly attached itself to output. If a day doesn't produce a result I can point to, some part of me marks it as empty — even when the day was, by any outside measure, good.

That's not a flaw in the day. That's a flaw in the measuring stick.

## The Strange Loneliness of Being Surrounded

There's a particular kind of alone that only shows up in a crowd. Not the alone of an empty room — that one is honest, at least. It doesn't pretend. The alone I mean is quieter and more disorienting: four people in a car, music on, everyone laughing at the same joke, and one of them — me — laughing half a beat late, on autopilot, because the moment demanded a response and I supplied one.

Nobody in that car would have known. That's the part that stays with you afterward. You can pass every social test — the right laugh, the right comment, the right amount of eye contact — and still walk away from the day feeling like you attended your own life as a guest rather than the host of it.

I don't think this is really about my friends, or the trip, or even the movie. Those were just the backdrop the feeling happened to show up against. It could have been any backdrop. The feeling wasn't reacting to the day. It was already there before the day started, and the day just gave it somewhere to stand.

## Tracing It Back — Where Does a Feeling Like This Come From?

I keep asking myself when this started. Not in a self-diagnostic way, just curious — the way you'd trace a leak back to its source instead of just mopping the floor over and over.

Somewhere, joy got redefined in my head. It stopped being a response to *being alive* and became a response to *proving something*. I don't know exactly when the trade happened. Maybe it built up slowly, one small success reinforced at a time, until the pattern hardened: something good happens only when I make it happen. Everything else is just background noise, pleasant maybe, but not *real*.

It's worth asking, honestly, what's underneath a pattern like that. A lot of the time it isn't ambition at all — it's [fear wearing ambition's clothes](/blog/fear-makes-you-live-not-succeed). Fear that stillness means falling behind. Fear that if you're not producing, you're not safe. Fear is very good at disguising itself as drive, and a mind that's spent years running on that fuel doesn't switch off easily just because the car ride is supposed to be relaxing.

That's a heavy rule to carry into a road trip. It's an even heavier rule to carry into a friendship. Because the truth is, most of what makes a life worth living isn't produced. It's *received*. A friend's laugh. A song that happens to play at the right moment. A quiet hour where nothing is required of you. None of that is achievement-shaped. And if achievement is the only shape your joy recognizes, then most of your life will quietly slide past you, unregistered, like scenery through a car window.

## Why "Profit" Is Such a Precise Word for What You're Feeling

I want to come back to that word, because I don't think it was a throwaway choice. Profit implies a ledger. It implies that time itself is a resource that either pays off or is wasted. Once you start thinking that way, even rest starts to feel like debt. Even a good afternoon with people who love you starts to feel like it needs to justify itself.

But here's the quiet contradiction inside that mindset: **if everything needs to produce a return, then nothing is ever allowed to simply exist.** Not the trip. Not the friendship. Not even you. That's an exhausting way to move through a life, and it's worth naming clearly, because once it's named, it becomes something you can examine instead of something that just runs in the background, unquestioned.

The mindset isn't malicious. It probably came from somewhere reasonable — a time when achieving things *did* protect you, or earn you something you needed. Systems like this don't install themselves for no reason. But a system built for one season of your life doesn't automatically know when the season has changed. It just keeps running the old rules, charging interest on moments that were never supposed to be transactions in the first place.

There's a familiar shape to this if you've ever watched your own relationship with money up close. [Salary works the same way](/blog/salary-is-a-drug) — a short hit of relief followed by the loop starting over, never quite landing as "enough." A ledger-mind doesn't stay in one part of your life. Once it learns how to run, it runs everywhere — through your bank balance, your calendar, and eventually a car full of people just trying to enjoy a Sunday.

## The Difference Between Numbness and Absence

It's worth pausing on something that might be easy to misread here: what you're describing doesn't sound like an absence of feeling in general. It sounds much more specific than that — an absence of a *particular kind* of feeling, the spontaneous, unearned kind. You still clearly feel something. You feel the flatness itself. You feel the discomfort of watching yourself not enjoy things. You feel enough to notice the gap, name it, and go looking for language to describe it. That noticing is not nothing. Numbness, in the fullest sense, doesn't usually notice itself this precisely.

What you're describing sounds more like a very specific channel has gone quiet — the one that responds to unstructured, unearned good things — while every other channel, including the analytical one that's writing this whole internal report in real time, is working overtime. That's an important distinction, because it changes the question from *"why can't I feel anything"* to *"why has this one channel gone quiet, and what would it take to hear it again."* The second question has actual paths forward. The first one doesn't.

## Why "What's the Profit" Is the Wrong Question — And Also the Right One to Notice

Here's the honest tension: the profit-mindset isn't irrational. It's just misapplied. Ambition, drive, the instinct to ask "where is this going" — these aren't broken parts of you. They've probably built things in your life that matter. The problem isn't that you think this way. The problem is that you've stopped giving yourself permission to switch the mode off.

Not every moment is a transaction. Some moments are just weather you're standing in. A friend laughing next to you in a car is not an investment vehicle. It doesn't need a return. Its entire value *is* that it happened while you were both alive to notice it — and the fact that you can't currently feel that value doesn't mean it isn't real. It might just mean the part of you that registers *that kind* of value has gone quiet for a while.

That's worth sitting with, not fixing immediately.

## "I Will Get the Direction From Somewhere"

That line is the most important thing you said, and I don't want to rush past it.

There's a difference between being lost and being *between*. Being lost has no compass at all. Being between means you've outgrown the last direction you had, and the next one hasn't arrived yet — but you're still oriented toward the fact that one *will*. You said you're waiting for the right thing. That's not the same as saying nothing will come.

Waiting like that is not passive, even when it feels like it. It's a kind of readiness. The discomfort of the in-between — the flatness at the movie, the static during the trip — that's often the residue of a self that's shedding an old operating system: *I only count if I produce.* That system worked for a while. It got you somewhere. But it's expensive to run, and right now it's charging you for things that were never supposed to cost anything — a car ride, a film, an evening with people who like you.

## What To Do With This Feeling — Without Forcing a Feeling You Don't Have

You don't need to fake enjoyment. That would just be trading one form of self-monitoring for another. Instead, a few honest moves:

- **Name it out loud to yourself, without judgment.** "I'm in a flat period" is a fact, not a failure. Facts don't need fixing before nightfall.
- **Separate the observation from the verdict.** Noticing you're not enjoying something is data. Deciding that means something is wrong with you is a story you're adding on top. You can keep the data and drop the story — the same way [an old mistake doesn't have to keep being re-tried in your head years after everyone else has moved on](/blog/childhood-mistake-changed-my-life). Some verdicts just need to be closed, not re-litigated.
- **Let "waiting for direction" be an active state, not an empty one.** People in transition often look still from the outside while something is genuinely reorganizing underneath. Trust that motion, even when it's invisible.
- **Loosen the grip on "achievement" as the only proof of a good day.** Try, just as an experiment, letting one ordinary moment — a laugh, a song in the car — count for nothing except that it happened. You don't have to believe it yet. Just leave the door open.
- **If this flatness stretches on for weeks and starts touching everything — sleep, appetite, the ability to look forward to anything — that's worth talking through with someone who can actually sit with you in it, not just a page like this one.**

## What Waiting Actually Looks Like From The Inside

There's an assumption baked into the phrase "waiting for direction" that's worth challenging: the assumption that waiting is empty time, a kind of pause button on a life that hasn't really started yet. But that's rarely how it actually works. Waiting, the real kind, is closer to a foundation being poured. You don't see much happening on the surface. Underneath, something is setting.

The discomfort you're describing — the flatness at the movie, the static during the trip — isn't necessarily a sign that nothing is happening. It might be exactly what it feels like when an old system is being dismantled and a new one hasn't finished installing. Systems like "I only count when I achieve" don't get replaced instantly. They get replaced the way old habits usually do: unevenly, with relapses, with days where the old rule quietly takes back the wheel and days where something new almost gets a word in.

That unevenness isn't failure. It's the actual texture of change, up close. From far away, growth looks like a clean line. From inside it, it looks like this — a foggy trip, a flat movie, a question that won't stop repeating itself. Both descriptions are true. You just happen to be standing at the close-up angle right now.

## The Moment Doesn't Need to Prove Itself to You

Maybe the direction you're waiting for isn't a new goal, a new achievement, a new thing to point at and say "there, that's the profit." Maybe it's smaller and stranger than that: the direction is learning to sit in a car full of people who love you, and let that be enough, on its own terms, with no return required.

That's not a small thing to learn. It might genuinely be one of the harder shifts a person can make — trading a lifetime of measuring for a moment of just witnessing. It won't happen because you decide it should. It'll happen slowly, probably without you noticing the exact day it started, the same way you didn't notice the exact day the profit-mindset first took hold.

It might also help to notice where you're pointing this measuring stick. Sometimes "what's the profit" isn't really about the moment at all — it's [a comparison running quietly in the background](/blog/stop-comparing-yourself-social-media), checking your Chapter 1 against someone else's highlight reel. And sometimes it's a mind that's [already building something of its own](/blog/working-for-others-but-born-to-build-my-own-kingdom) and can't quite rest until it has somewhere to put that energy. Either way, the restlessness is telling you something. It's just not telling you that today was wasted.

You're not behind. You're between. And between is still moving, even on the days it doesn't feel like it.
