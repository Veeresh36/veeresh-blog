# 10 AI Prompts Every Job Seeker Needs in 2026 (Photo, Resume, LinkedIn & Interview Prep)

**Published:** July 31, 2026 · **13 min read** · By [Veeresh Bashetti](https://veereshbashetti.com/about)

---

## Why "Write Me a Resume" Never Works

I build production systems for a living, not resumes — but I've watched enough people around me job-hunt in the last year to notice the same pattern every time. Someone opens ChatGPT, types "make my resume better," gets back a document that *sounds* impressive, and hears nothing back from twenty applications. The problem was never the AI. It was the prompt.

A vague instruction gets a vague, generic result — the model has no idea what role you're targeting, what actually made you good at your last job, or what the specific posting is asking for. A specific prompt, with a clear role, a real task, and your actual data, gets you something you could genuinely send out today.

This is the prep list I'd hand a friend starting a job search right now — in the order I'd actually use it, starting with the one thing almost nobody thinks to prompt for: the photo.

## 1. The Professional Passport / ID-Style Photo Prompt

Most job-search guides skip straight to the resume and ignore the photo entirely — but a passport, visa, or ID-style photo is still something almost everyone needs at some point during a job search or relocation, and a phone selfie rarely meets the spec. AI tools have gotten good enough at this specific, narrow task that a proper studio visit isn't always necessary anymore.

```
Turn this selfie into an official passport/ID-style photo.
Preserve my exact facial identity, skin texture, and features —
do not beautify, smooth, or alter my face in any way.

Requirements:
- Plain white or light-grey background, no shadows
- Head centered, facing forward, neutral expression, mouth closed
- Even, soft studio-style lighting
- Sharp focus on eyes, eyebrows, and hairline
- Simple, formal clothing, no logos or accessories
- Realistic skin tone and natural texture — no filters

Output as a clean, front-facing headshot suitable for an
official document photo.
```

**Why this works:** the two lines that matter most are "preserve my exact facial identity" and "no filters." Without them, image AI tends to smooth skin and subtly reshape features — which is exactly what most passport and visa authorities reject on inspection. Whatever tool you use, always compare the final result against your specific country's official photo requirements (head size ratio, exact background color, allowed expression) before submitting it anywhere official — these specs vary by country and do get updated.

## 2. The LinkedIn-Ready Professional Headshot Prompt

A passport photo and a great LinkedIn photo aren't the same brief — one needs to be neutral and compliant, the other needs to look approachable and current. According to LinkedIn's own data, profiles with a professional photo get dramatically more views and far more connection requests than profiles without one, which makes this one of the highest-leverage single edits in your whole profile.

```
Turn this selfie into a professional headshot suitable for a
LinkedIn profile photo. Preserve my exact identity and natural
skin tone — do not beautify or alter my face.

Requirements:
- Soft, neutral-colored or softly blurred background
- Warm, even lighting with a natural catchlight in the eyes
- Confident, approachable expression — slight natural smile
- Business-appropriate attire
- Square (1:1) crop, head and shoulders framed
- Sharp, realistic, no artificial smoothing
```

**Why this works:** the "slight natural smile" and "approachable" instructions are what separate a LinkedIn photo from a passport photo — recruiters scroll past dozens of profiles a day, and a neutral-but-warm expression reads as more trustworthy than a stiff, official-looking one.

## 3. The ATS Keyword & Gap Audit Prompt (Do This Before Rewriting Anything)

Most job postings today pass through an Applicant Tracking System before a human ever sees them, and modern ATS platforms increasingly use AI-based semantic matching rather than simple keyword counting — meaning the goal is honest alignment with the job description, not keyword stuffing. This prompt is step one of the chain: diagnose before you rewrite.

```
You are an ATS and hiring-manager specialist reviewing my resume
against a specific job description.

Compare the two and list:
1. Must-have hard skills present in the JD but missing/weak in my resume
2. Must-have soft skills present in the JD but missing/weak in my resume
3. Repeated keywords or phrases the JD emphasizes
4. Resume bullets that are vague, task-based, or missing metrics
5. Any red flags a recruiter might hesitate on (gaps, inconsistent
   dates, generic titles)

Then give me exactly 5 changes to make first, ranked by impact.

Job description: [PASTE JOB DESCRIPTION]
My resume: [PASTE RESUME]
```

**Why this works:** asking for a ranked list of "5 changes first" stops the AI from dumping generic advice — it forces prioritization, which is what you actually need when you're applying to multiple roles under time pressure.

## 4. The Achievement Rewrite Prompt (Turning Duties Into Results)

Once you know which bullets are weak, the fix is rewriting them from a list of *duties* into a list of *results* — with real numbers wherever you have them.

```
Act as a professional resume writer. Rewrite the bullets below
for a [target job title] role.

Requirements:
- Keep everything truthful — do not invent numbers, tools, or
  responsibilities I didn't mention
- Start each bullet with a strong action verb
- Focus on outcomes and impact, not daily tasks
- Keep each bullet under 2 lines
- Where I've given a number or result, keep it exact

My current bullets: [PASTE BULLETS]
Context/achievements to draw from: [PASTE NOTES]
```

**Why this works:** the "do not invent" instruction is the single most important line in this whole article. AI resume tools will happily fabricate a "40% efficiency improvement" if you let them — and a fabricated metric that falls apart under interview questioning does far more damage than a modest, honest one.

## 5. The Professional Summary & Resume Headline Prompt

Recruiters reportedly spend only a few seconds on a first resume scan, which is exactly why the top three or four lines matter more than any other section.

```
Write 3 short professional summary options (3–4 lines each)
for a [target job title] role with [X] years of experience in
[industry].

Requirements:
- Mention years of experience, 2–3 core strengths, and the target role
- Include my strongest measurable achievement: [ACHIEVEMENT]
- No clichés like "hardworking," "team player," or "results-oriented"
- Make each of the 3 versions slightly different in tone —
  one direct, one warmer, one more technical
```

**Why this works:** asking for three tonal variants, rather than one, lets you actually pick the version that sounds like you — instead of settling for whatever the model generates first.

## 6. The Honest Cover Letter Prompt

A cover letter's job isn't to repeat your resume — it's to connect your specific background to this specific company and role, in a way a generic template can't fake.

```
You are a cover letter writer. Write a concise, professional
cover letter for the role below.

Structure:
1. A short, confident opening stating who I am and what role
   I'm applying for
2. 2–3 sentences connecting my real experience to the job's
   key requirements, with one concrete example
3. A short note on why this specific company interests me,
   tied to something real about their mission or work
4. A confident closing inviting further conversation

Keep it under 300 words. Do not invent experience I haven't
given you.

Job description: [PASTE JOB DESCRIPTION]
My background: [PASTE KEY EXPERIENCE / RESUME]
Why this company: [YOUR REAL REASON]
```

**Why this works:** the "why this company" input is the one section AI genuinely can't fake convincingly on its own — you have to supply the real reason, and that's exactly the part hiring managers can tell apart from a template.

## 7. The LinkedIn Headline & About Section Prompt

LinkedIn's search ranks profiles by keyword match in the headline, About section, and experience entries — so a narrative-only profile, however well written, can quietly lose to a search-optimized one.

```
Help me write a LinkedIn headline and About section for a
[target job title] role in [industry].

Headline: under 220 characters, keyword-rich for recruiter
search, not just a job title — include 2–3 specific skills
or specialties.

About section: 3 short paragraphs.
1. Who I am and what I do, in plain language
2. My strongest, most specific achievement with real numbers
3. What I'm looking for next, and a soft call to action

Keep the tone confident and human, not robotic or full of buzzwords.

My background: [PASTE KEY EXPERIENCE]
```

**Why this works:** treating the headline as a search field, not just a title, is the mental shift that actually gets a profile surfaced in more recruiter searches.

## 8. The Mock Interview & Feedback Prompt

Practicing out loud against real questions — with honest feedback on the answer, not just the question — is where most interview prep falls short when people just read question lists silently.

```
Conduct a mock interview for a [job title] role at a
[company type/industry] company.

Ask me one interview question at a time — a mix of behavioral
and role-specific technical questions. After each answer I give:
1. Rate the answer's clarity and structure
2. Point out anything vague or unsupported by evidence
3. Suggest one specific way to strengthen it (e.g., add a
   number, tighten the STAR structure, cut filler)

Wait for my answer before giving feedback or moving to the
next question.
```

**Why this works:** the "wait for my answer" instruction stops the model from dumping all questions and model answers at once — it forces an actual back-and-forth, which is a far closer simulation of a real interview than reading a static list.

## 9. The Salary Negotiation Prep Prompt

Negotiation is the step people skip most often out of discomfort — but going in with researched numbers and a calm script changes the conversation completely.

```
Help me prepare for a salary negotiation for a [job title] role
in [city/country], with [X] years of experience.

1. Based on typical ranges for this role, seniority, and location,
   suggest a realistic target range (note that you may not have
   live data, so tell me what to verify myself)
2. Write a short, confident script for stating my target number
3. Write 2 responses for common pushback: "that's above our
   budget" and "we need to check with the team"
4. Keep the tone collaborative, not confrontational
```

**Why this works:** explicitly asking the AI to flag where its salary data might be outdated keeps you from anchoring on a number you haven't verified yourself — always cross-check against a current source like Glassdoor, Levels.fyi, or a recent job posting in your exact market before you use a number in a real negotiation.

## 10. The Post-Interview Thank-You & Follow-Up Prompt

The follow-up note is small, easy to forget under stress, and one of the few remaining chances to reinforce something you wish you'd said better in the room.

```
Write a short, genuine thank-you email to send after my
interview for the [job title] role.

Include:
1. Thanks for their time, mentioning [interviewer's name] and
   one specific thing discussed in the interview
2. One brief line reinforcing why I'm a strong fit, tied to
   something we discussed
3. A short, confident closing — no over-eagerness, no generic
   filler like "looking forward to hearing from you soon!"

Keep it under 120 words. Conversational, not stiff.
```

**Why this works:** referencing one specific moment from the actual conversation is what makes a thank-you note read as genuine instead of a template — and it's the one detail AI can't supply for you, so always fill it in yourself before sending.

## A Quick Word on Using These Responsibly

Every prompt above is written around one rule: the AI works from what you actually give it, not what it guesses. That means:

- Never let AI invent a number, a title, or an achievement you didn't have — it will surface in an interview, and it's not worth the risk.
- Treat AI output as a strong first draft, not a final answer — read it back in your own voice before sending anything.
- Be mindful of what personal information you paste into a public chatbot — avoid sensitive identifiers, and check your AI tool's data-training settings if that matters to you..
- For anything official — a passport, visa, or government ID photo — always verify the AI output against your specific country's current photo specification before submitting it.
