# I Spent ₹13,000 of My Scholarship Money on a CPU. It's Still Sitting in the Corner of My Room.

**Published:** May 27, 2026 · **10 min read** · By [Veeresh Bashetti](https://veereshbashetti.com/about)

There is a specific kind of regret that does not go away.

Not the regret of failing an exam. Not the regret of saying the wrong thing to someone. Those kinds fade.

This one is different.

This kind sits in the corner of your room. It collects dust. And every time you walk past it, it quietly reminds you of the exact moment you chose excitement over sense.

For me, that reminder is a second-hand CPU I bought for ₹13,000.

I have not used it since the week I got it.

---

## Where the Money Actually Came From

Before I tell you what happened, I need to be honest about something. Because this is not just a story about making a bad purchase.

₹10,000 of that money came from my scholarship. Money that was supposed to go toward my education, my future, my stability.

The other ₹3,000 came from my mom.

She did not ask me a single question. She just gave it. Quietly. The way parents do when they trust you without needing proof.

That is the part that still stings.

Money can be earned back. But when your mother hands over her savings because she believes in you — and you spend it on something you will never use — that is a different kind of debt. The kind that doesn't show up in your bank account.

If you want to understand the [biggest money mistakes people make in their 20s](/blog/money-mistakes-20s), start here: it is almost never about the math. It is about whose money you are spending, and why.

---

## The Story: How I Talked Myself Into It

Let me paint you the scene.

I was deep in a YouTube spiral. Developer setup videos. That corner of the internet where everyone has ultrawide monitors, ambient lighting, custom mechanical keyboards, and machines that look like they belong in a sci-fi film.

I was watching these videos and feeling something I now recognise as dangerous: *inadequacy dressed up as ambition.*

I wasn't thinking, "I want that because it looks cool." I was thinking, "I need that to become the developer I want to be."

Then I saw a listing on OLX. A used CPU, decent specs, at what felt like a steal of a price. And my brain, which had already decided to buy something, immediately got to work building a case.

Here is exactly what I told myself, word for word:

*"This will improve my productivity dramatically."*
*"I can run heavier builds, compile code faster."*
*"It's second-hand — I'm actually being financially smart."*
*"If I don't act now, someone else will grab it."*
*"This is an investment in my career."*

Every single one of these sounds completely reasonable. That is the trap.

Psychologists call it **post-hoc rationalisation** — your brain decides to do something emotionally first, then builds the logical argument afterward to justify it. You don't reason your way into the purchase. You feel your way in, then reason your way into convincing yourself it was reasonable.

I have written more about this pattern in my post on the [developer setup trap](/blog/developer-setup-trap) — the specific way tech people convince themselves that hardware is the missing ingredient.

---

## What Actually Happened After I Bought It

Day one: I opened the package. I tested it. I felt the clean dopamine rush of owning something new.

Day three: I had lectures. Assignments. Life.

Week two: The CPU was in a box.

Week four: The box was on a shelf.

Month two: The shelf had become a graveyard for things I bought with energy I no longer had.

Within six weeks, the whole story was over. The excitement had completely evaporated. What remained was just the arithmetic: ₹13,000 gone. Nothing solved. No problem that didn't already have a solution. No productivity gained.

But here is what surprised me — the cost did not end there.

Every time money was tight (which, as a student, is most of the time), I thought about that CPU. Every time I had to skip buying something actually useful — a book, a course, a tool I was actively using — I thought about that CPU. Every time I felt guilty about not being more disciplined with my finances, I thought about that CPU.

Impulsive purchases don't just take your money once. They tax you repeatedly, in the form of guilt that returns whenever your financial situation creates any friction at all.

## Why This Happens to So Many of Us in Our 20s

I want to be clear about something: the people who make these mistakes are not irresponsible. They are not careless. Most of them are actually trying really hard.

The problem is a set of emotional pressures that are completely specific to your 20s — and that nobody adequately warns you about.

**You are still figuring out who you are.**

In your 20s, you are building an identity. And purchases feel like declarations. Buying a developer setup feels like *becoming* a serious developer. Buying running shoes feels like *becoming* a fit person. Buying a camera feels like *becoming* a creative. The purchase substitutes for the actual work of becoming.

**Social media shows you thousands of highlight reels at once.**

You are not comparing yourself to your actual peers. You are comparing yourself to the curated best moments of thousands of people simultaneously. The result is a constant, low-level feeling that you are behind — and that the gap can be closed by buying the right thing.

This is why I wrote an entire post about [stopping the comparison spiral with people online](/blog/stop-comparing-yourself-social-media). It is one of the most expensive habits most young people don't even realise they have.

**You have just received money that feels "free."**

Scholarship money, a gift, a bonus, a refund — unexpected money does not feel the same as earned money. Research consistently shows that people spend windfalls faster and with less care than money they worked for. The moment I received that scholarship disbursement, it already felt partially spent in my mind.

**Purchases feel like progress when real progress feels slow.**

When you are learning but not yet earning, building but not yet launching, growing but not yet seeing results — a purchase gives you an immediate, tangible sense of forward movement. It is a fake progress signal that costs real money. I know this feeling well from [what I learned when my first project failed](/blog/first-project-failure-lessons) — the impulse to buy something is often loudest exactly when your actual work feels most stuck.

---

## The Gear Fallacy: Why Developers and Creators Are Especially Vulnerable

There is a specific trap in the tech and creative world that I want to name directly.

It is the belief that a better setup produces better work.

It doesn't.

The people building real things, shipping real products, solving real problems? Many of them are working on ₹30,000 laptops. On whatever they had available. The work comes from discipline, consistency, and showing up — not from what processor sits under the hood.

The setup you see in YouTube thumbnails is the *reward* of success. Not the cause of it.

I knew this. I had read it. I had even written about the [developer setup obsession](/blog/developer-setup-trap) once. And I still bought the CPU.

Because knowing something intellectually and believing it emotionally are two completely different things.

---

## The 5 Questions I Ask Myself Before Every Major Purchase Now

After the CPU, I built a simple filter. Before spending anything significant — anything above ₹2,000 — I make myself answer five questions. All five need a clean answer before the money moves.

**1. Will I still want this in 30 days?**

If you cannot wait 30 days, that is not urgency — that is impulse. Real needs survive a month of waiting. Emotional wants usually dissolve within 72 hours. I've seen [how well the 30-day rule actually works](/blog/30-day-rule-impulse-buying) when you apply it consistently.

**2. Am I solving a problem I actually have right now — or a problem I might have someday?**

The CPU was supposed to help with hypothetical future builds. My actual, current workflow had no problem that the CPU would fix. Speculative purchases are almost always regrets.

**3. Can my current setup already do this?**

Nine times out of ten, the answer is yes. We rarely outgrow our tools. We outgrow our discipline and habits — and no tool can fix that.

**4. Am I buying to *perform* something — or to *do* something?**

Buying a CPU to feel like a serious developer is performance. Buying a specific component because an active build requires it is doing. These are completely different purchases, even when they look identical from the outside.

**5. What would I think of this three months from now?**

Imagine your future self explaining this purchase to someone you respect. Does it sound like a smart decision? Or does it sound like an embarrassing story you'd rather not tell? That imagined moment of future perspective is one of the most powerful impulse-breaking tools I have found.

## What ₹13,000 Actually Buys When You Spend It Well

For a student in their 20s, ₹13,000 is genuinely significant money. Here is what that same amount could do:

- A quality course on DSA, system design, or full-stack development from Scaler, Coding Ninjas, or Udemy — with money left over
- Six months of hosting, a domain, and the tools to launch and maintain a real project
- Thirty or more books — finance, psychology, technical skills — that compound in value over a lifetime
- An emergency fund that covers two months of essential expenses, which removes financial anxiety from every decision you make afterward
- A single, high-quality peripheral you will actually use every day for years — a good mouse, a comfortable keyboard

None of these looks as exciting as a new CPU in a product listing. Every single one of them pays off longer.

And if you are ready to start [how I finally started budgeting properly](/blog/budgeting-for-beginners-india) — the post I wrote about that process is the most practical thing I've put together on this topic.

---

## One Expensive Mistake Does Not Define You. The Pattern Does.

If you are reading this and you recognise yourself somewhere in this story — an unused thing, a regretted purchase, scholarship money spent on something that didn't deserve it — I want to say something clearly:

The mistake is not the problem.

**The pattern is the problem.**

A single ₹13,000 mistake is tuition. Repeating it is a lifestyle.

The function of an expensive mistake is not to make you feel permanently stupid. It is to change the decision-making system that produced it. If you build better questions, better waiting periods, and honest awareness of your emotional triggers, the mistake will have taught you something that compounds in value over your entire financial life.

That is what this CPU cost me.

And slowly, quietly, over the months since — that is what it has been teaching me in return.

---

## The Dust Is the Teacher

That CPU is still in my room.

I have thought about selling it. But honestly? I have kept it there on purpose.

Every time I feel that familiar heat in my chest around a new piece of tech — a deal that feels urgent, a listing that feels like it will disappear — I look at it.

And I remember, very precisely, how convincing the logic sounded last time.

Your 20s will give you many lessons. Some arrive through wins. Some arrive through staring at a ₹13,000 piece of hardware in the corner of a room you pay rent for, trying to remember why you ever thought it was a good idea.

Those lessons? They do not leave.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is buying a second-hand CPU worth it in India?

It can be — but only when you have a real, immediate use for it right now, not a hypothetical future use. Before buying, check socket compatibility with your existing motherboard, verify the CPU's thermal history, and compare the second-hand price against the new price. If the difference is less than ₹2,000–3,000, just buy new for the warranty. The biggest mistake people make is buying second-hand hardware they don't have a complete build plan for.

### How do I stop making impulsive purchases in my 20s?

The most effective method is the 30-day wait rule — add the item to a wishlist, set a reminder for 30 days later, and only buy it if you still want it AND it fits your budget. Most impulse urges die within 72 hours. Other tactics that work: delete your saved card details from shopping sites, unsubscribe from deal alerts and tech newsletters, and ask yourself one honest question before every purchase: "Am I solving a problem I actually have right now?"

### What are the biggest money mistakes people make in their 20s?

The most common ones are lifestyle inflation (spending more as you earn more without saving the difference), impulse tech and gadget purchases, no emergency fund, investing before clearing high-interest debt, and spending on status symbols to impress people who aren't watching. The root cause behind all of them is almost always emotional — not mathematical. See our full breakdown of the biggest money mistakes in your 20s for more.

### How should students manage scholarship money wisely?

Treat scholarship money like a salary with non-negotiable rules. Allocate a fixed percentage to essentials (rent, food, transport, books), a small portion to personal growth (online courses, tools you actually use), and keep a minimum emergency buffer of one month's expenses. Never spend scholarship money on wants in the first 30 days of receiving it — the "windfall effect" makes us spend unexpected money much faster than earned money.

### Can emotional spending become a habit that you break?

Yes — but you have to identify your personal trigger first. Most emotional spenders buy when they feel stagnant, anxious about falling behind, or overstimulated by aspirational content on social media. The fix is not willpower — it is awareness. Keep a simple spending journal for one month: every time you feel the urge to buy something non-essential, write down what you were feeling right before. The pattern becomes very obvious, very fast.
